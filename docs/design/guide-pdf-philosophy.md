# Quiet Cartography

*The design philosophy behind the DayHop city guide PDFs. The renderer in
`services/cityGuides.js` is the canvas; every purchased copy is a print of
this movement.*

Quiet Cartography is the conviction that a place can be honoured by the way
it is drawn. It borrows its language from the two most trustworthy documents
outdoors people know — the topographic map and the park-entrance sign — and
treats them not as references but as ancestors. Contour lines, section
markers, coordinates, thin rules: the furniture of surveying, redeployed as
art. The work must feel like a field document produced by a master
draughtsman over countless patient evenings — every line weight deliberate,
every interval measured, nothing decorative that is not also structural.

Space is the first material. Paper (#FBF7EE) is not a background but a
landscape of its own, and the composition settles into it the way a lake
settles into a moraine — most of the page left breathing, the marks
gathering in one gravitational field. Density is earned through repetition,
never through clutter: a motif drawn once is an illustration, drawn thirty
times at meticulously varied intervals it becomes terrain. The eye should
wander the accumulation the way a hiker reads a slope.

Color is a fixed law, not a palette to browse. Deep ink (#102A2E) carries
authority — titles, marks of record. Moss (#3F6F5C) is the land itself —
the repeated line, the contour field, the quiet system. Coral (#E1502F)
appears exactly once per composition if it appears at all: the trail
marker, the single blaze that orients the whole page. Its scarcity is the
craftsmanship — one coral gesture placed with the care of a surveyor
driving a stake.

Typography behaves like signage and marginalia, never like a document.
Display lettering (National Park) stands the way a routed wooden sign
stands: confident, letterspaced, unhurried, small in count. Reading text
(Crimson) is the naturalist's notebook hand — warm, serifed, set narrow and
modest. Numbers, coordinates, and reference markers are set tiny and exact,
clinical instruments that prove the page was measured. No element shouts;
hierarchy is achieved by scale and interval, the product of painstaking
calibration rather than weight.

Each composition carries a buried signature: the geography of its subject
abstracted into the motif itself — a river's meander, a kettle lake's
rings, an escarpment's fall line. It is never labeled as such. Someone who
knows the land should feel a flicker of recognition; everyone else simply
sees a beautiful system of marks. This restraint is the movement's soul:
the reference enriches without announcing, the way a contour map never
mentions that the valley is beautiful.

The standard is museum print, not office document. Alignments hold to the
point. Margins are generous and constant. Nothing overlaps, nothing
crowds, nothing is left to a default. The finished page should look as if
it took a season of evenings at a drafting table — because in every way
that matters, it did.
